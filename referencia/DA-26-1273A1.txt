MEMORANDUM OPINION AND ORDER

I. INTRODUCTION
1. In this Memorandum Opinion and Order (Order), the Space Bureau (Bureau) addresses issues of law and policy raised in petitions and comments filed on the record regarding the application filed by Space Exploration Holdings LLC (SpaceX) for authority to deploy and operate its proposed Starmind constellation consisting of one million non-geostationary satellite orbit (NGSO) satellites operating as orbital data centers (ODCs) in low-Earth orbit (LEO) (Starmind). See Space Exploration Holdings, LLC, Application for Authority to Launch and Operate the SpaceX Orbital Data Center System, ICFS File No. SAT-LOA-20260108-00016 (filed Jan. 20, 2026) (Application). 
  We find that grant of this application will serve the public interest by permitting SpaceX to pioneer emergent technology that advances American leadership in space and artificial intelligence.   The space economy continues to grow, and technologies once confined to the ground—like servicing, manufacturing, and now artificial intelligence—are proliferating in space.  These emerging technologies require access to radiofrequency spectrum to support backup and emergency mission operations.  
II. BACKGROUND
2. On January 30, 2026, SpaceX filed an application requesting authority for backup and emergency use of radiofrequency communications to support an NGSO constellation of one million ODCs. Id.  See Grant, supra, for the authorized orbital parameters and frequencies for the Starmind constellation.
  The Application was accepted for filing and placed on public notice. Space Bureau Accepts for Filing SpaceX’s Application for Orbital Data Centers, Public Notice, DA 26-113 (rel. Feb 4, 2026).  Unless otherwise specified, all citations to petitions, comments, letters, and other filings in this Order can be located in the record for this application in the International Communications Filing System (ICFS), ICFS File No. SAT-LOA-20260108-00016.  
  SpaceX’s Application received a substantial number of comments from the public, including petitions to deny filed by Kuiper Systems LLC (Amazon Leo), Viasat Inc. (Viasat), and the American Astronomical Society (AAS). Petition to Deny of Kuiper Systems LLC (filed Mar. 6, 2026) (Amazon Petition); Petition to Deny of Viasat Inc. (filed Mar. 6, 2026) (Viasat Petition); Petition to Deny of the American Astronomical Society (filed Mar. 6, 2026) (AAS Petition).  SpaceX filed a consolidated opposition to petitions and comments.  See Consolidated Opposition to Petitions and Response to Comments of Space Explorations Holdings, LLC (filed Mar. 16, 2026) (SpaceX Response).
  
III. DISCUSSION
3. Upon review of the record, we find that grant of the Starmind application serves the public interest, convenience, and necessity.  Below, we address the various outstanding issues raised by the record.  First, we address concerns on the record regarding potential radiofrequency interference posed by SpaceX’s system.  Next, we turn to SpaceX’s waiver requests and concerns on the record regarding SpaceX’s orbital debris mitigation plan.  Finally, we dismiss comments and concerns raised regarding topics over which the Commission has no authority, including environmental concerns regarding the Starmind constellation and potential impacts of the Starmind constellation on optical astronomy.
A. Radiofrequency Interference
4. Under the Communications Act of 1934, as amended, “[n]o person shall use or operate any apparatus for the transmission of energy or communications or signals by radio” from or within the areas specified in section 301 or on a mobile station within the jurisdiction of the United States without a license issued by the Commission. 47 U.S.C. § 301.
  The Commission therefore licenses the deployment and operation of space stations and earth stations as it generally pertains to the communications between them. 47 CFR § 25.102(a).  Under the Commission’s rules, a “space station” is a “station located on an object which is beyond, is intended to go beyond, or has been beyond, the major portion of the Earth’s atmosphere.”  An “earth station” is a “station located either on the Earth’s surface or within the major portion of Earth's atmosphere and intended for communication:  (1) With one or more space stations; or (2) With one or more stations of the same kind by means of one or more reflecting satellites or other objects in space.”  47 CFR §§ 2.1, 25.103.
  In the case of SpaceX’s application, these communications include backup and emergency telemetry, tracking, and command (TT&C) communications between the proposed space stations and gateway earth stations that are currently authorized to conduct communications with SpaceX’s previously-authorized first- and second-generation Starlink constellations. 
5. SpaceX requests authority for radiofrequency communications in the Ka-band frequencies of 18.8-19.3 GHz (space-to-Earth) and 28.6-29.1 GHz (Earth-to-space). See e.g., SpaceX Application, Legal Narrative at 6.  While SpaceX contends that these TT&C operations fall within the Commission’s definition of fixed-satellite service (FSS), the Commission is currently seeking comment on whether the definition of FSS in our rules permits standalone TT&C operations in frequencies allocated for FSS in a pending rulemaking.  See SpaceX May 29, 2026 Letter at 2; Spectrum Abundance for Weird Space Stuff, Notice of Proposed Rulemaking, FCC 26-13, at paras. 31-33 (rel. Mar. 5, 2026) (WSS NPRM).  We conclude there is good cause in this instance to waive, on our own motion, the U.S. Table of Frequency Allocations (U.S. Table) in section 2.106(a) of the Commission’s rules to permit SpaceX to conduct TT&C in these bands.  47 CFR § 2.106(a). 
  SpaceX states that it does not plan to operate Starmind satellites in the Ka-band except as necessary to support limited backup and emergency operations because the primary communications for each ODC will be via optical intersatellite links (ISLs) with SpaceX’s Starlink constellations. See Letter from Madeleine Chang, Satellite Policy Manager, SpaceX, to Marlene H. Dortch, Secretary, FCC at 3-4 (filed Sep. 16, 2026) (SpaceX September 16, 2026 Letter); see also Application, Legal Narrative at 6 (“This system will primarily rely upon optical inter-satellite links, which may connect among or between those satellites and satellites in SpaceX’s first- and second-generation Starlink system.”). 
  In the event that SpaceX will communicate using Ka-band frequencies authorized in this grant, it has committed to do so solely on an unprotected and non-interference basis. See Application, Legal Narrative at 1-2; Application, Technical Attachment at 2; Letter from Cecilia Tenge-Rietberg, Senior Satellite Policy Manager, Space Exploration Technologies Corp., to Marlene H. Dortch, Secretary, FCC at 1 (filed May 29, 2026) (SpaceX May 29, 2026 Letter).  
  We condition our authorization of SpaceX’s radiofrequency communications on SpaceX conducting perations on an unprotected, non-interference basis and on SpaceX completing coordination with Federal operators.  We agree with SpaceX that it is unlikely that an outage of the optical communications system will occur at sufficient scale to require SpaceX to communicate using Ka-band spectrum on all one million satellites simultaneously.  Additionally, SpaceX states that in the unlikely event that a Starmind satellite will need to operate in the Ka-band, it will communicate directly with licensed gateway earth stations operating within the authorized and coordinated envelope of SpaceX Gen1 and Gen2 systems. SpaceX September 16, 2026 Letter at 4.  SpaceX must obtain the required earth station authorizations prior to communicating with any gateway earth stations with the Starmind satellites.
 We find that these factors and the grant condition address concerns that the aggregate interference from one million Starmind satellites operating simultaneously will cause interference into other satellite and terrestrial systems. See e.g., Viasat Petition at 3-7; Comments of WISPA (filed Mar. 6, 2026) (WISPA Comments).  However, to ensure no harmful interference occurs, we also condition this grant on SpaceX reporting, on a semiannual basis, the number of satellites that communicated with earth stations using Ka-band frequencies.  See Grant, supra, condition 6e.
  We are also unpersuaded by arguments that the Starmind constellation itself will reflect radio waves and cause interference into other radiofrequency services, See e.g., Comments of the Square Kilometer Array Observatory at 1-2 (filed Mar. 5, 2026) (SKAO Comments).
 because these comments are not specific to SpaceX’s proposed radiofrequency operations and provide no technical support for claims of potential interference from the alleged potential reflection of radio waves by the Starmind satellites or other unintentional emissions.  
B. Waiver Requests
6. Waiver Standard.  Generally, the Commission may waive any rule for good cause shown. 47 CFR § 1.3. 
  Waiver is appropriate where the particular facts make strict compliance inconsistent with the public interest. Northeast Cellular Tel. Co. v. FCC, 897 F.2d 1164, 1166 (D.C. Cir. 1990). 
  In making this determination, we may take into account considerations of hardship, equity, or more effective implementation of overall policy on an individual basis. WAIT Radio v. FCC, 418 F.2d 1153, 1159 (D.C. Cir. 1969), cert. denied, 409 U.S. 1027 (1972); Northeast Cellular, 897 F.2d at 1166. 
  Waiver is therefore appropriate if special circumstances warrant a deviation from the general rule and such deviation will serve the public interest. Northeast Cellular, 897 F.2d at 1166. 
  
7. SpaceX requests waivers of the Commission’s processing round procedures and surety bond and milestone requirements.  In general, we note many commenters object to SpaceX’s waiver requests on the whole, arguing that the combination of processing round, milestone, and bond waiver requests eliminates all regulatory guardrails on an untested technology, See e.g., Comments of Secure World Foundation at 2 (filed Mar. 6, 2026) (SWF Comments); Comments of Dark Sky International at 14-16 (filed Mar. 6, 2026) (Dark Sky Comments). 
 but the Commission has repeatedly waived these rules, including for innovative and untested technologies and when operations would be performed on an unprotected, non-interference basis without precluding other users of the same spectrum. See e.g., Reflect Orbital, Inc., Application for Authority to Construct, Launch, and Operate a Non-Geostationary Satellite in the Space Operation and Space Research Services, Authorization and Order, DA-26-706 (SB rel. Jul. 9, 2026) (Reflect Orbital Grant and Order); Impulse Space Inc., Grant Stamp, ICFS File No. SAT-LOA-20250406-00093 (granted Aug. 15, 2025); Basalt Technologies Corp., Grant Stamp, ICFS File No. SAT-LOA-20250310-00067 (granted-in-part/deferred-in-part Jan. 23, 2026); Momentus Space LLC, Grant Stamp, ICFS File No. SAT-LOA-20250626-00122 (granted Jan. 30, 2026); Muon Space Inc., Grant Stamp, ICFS File No. SAT-LOA-20251119-00332 (granted May 1, 2026). 
  We grant these waivers in accordance with Space Bureau policy and impose conditions to ensure protection of other systems and prevent spectrum warehousing, so grant of these waiver requests does not undermine the purpose of the Commission’s rules. Some commenters allege that there are too many waiver requests associated with this application, and that a large number of waiver requests is detrimental to the regulatory process.  See e.g., Dark Sky Comments; SWF Comments; Comments of Magellan; Comments of Accademia Nazionale dei Lincei; Comments of Angus Burns.  Nothing in the Commission’s rules, however, sets a limit on the number of waiver requests that may be included in an application, and innovative operations in space communications may not fit neatly within existing Commission rules, therefore requiring requests for rule waivers.

8. NGSO Processing Round Procedures.  We grant SpaceX’s request for waiver of the Commission’s processing round procedures in sections 25.156 and 25.157 for its proposed communications in the Ka-band. Application, Waiver Requests at 1-3; 47 CFR §§ 25.156, 25.157.  Sections 25.156 and 25.157 of the Commission’s rules set forth procedures for consideration of applications for NGSO satellite authorizations.  See 47 CFR §§ 25.156(d)(1), 25.157.  In most cases under the Commission’s current rules, applications not filed in response to a public notice initiating a processing round will be considered lead applications, and the Commission will initiate a new processing round and establish a cut-off date for the filing of competing applications.  47 CFR §§ 25.157(b), (i), 25.157(c)(2).
  SpaceX contends that its limited backup operations on a non-interference basis will not preclude other operators from using these bands, and stresses that its ODC system will have the ability to reposition beams and reroute traffic to prevent interference using a combination of network management strategies. See Application, Waiver Requests at 2 (stating that SpaceX will use a “combination of beam steering, optical links, and a uniquely flexible on-orbit network architecture.”). 
  These strategies, SpaceX contends, will allow it to protect systems authorized in previous and future processing rounds.  We find that granting the requested waivers is technically and legally justified and will not preclude other operations in these bands.  Under this waiver, SpaceX does not have any priority status associated with processing round procedures and accordingly must protect operators authorized in previous processing rounds and coordinate any existing or future uses of these bands with all existing and future users of these bands.

The purpose of the surety bond and milestone requirements is to encourage satellite operators to launch spacecraft expeditiously or surrender an authorization early if necessary, thereby deterring warehousing of spectrum and orbital resources.  Review of Licensing and Operating Rules for Satellite Services, Second Report and Order, 30 FCC Rcd 14713, 14742, para. 74 (2015). See Application, Waiver Requests at 3-4. See Application, Waiver requests at 3-4; Letter from Madeleine Chang, Satellite Policy Manager, Space Exploration Technologies Corp., to Marlene H.  it is in the public interest to grant a waiver of the surety bond and milestone requirements under part 25 for SpaceX’s system. See 47 CFR § 25.165.  See also Application, Waiver Requests at 2-3.

Space Modernization R&O and FNPRM at paras. 224-263.
  Given the Commission has adopted these new rules and the Space Bureau has released a public notice implementing them, we find it is also in the public interest to grant SpaceX’s request for waiver. 
C. Orbital Debris Mitigation
10. The Commission adopted rules in 2004 requiring all applicants for space station authorizations to disclose the strategies that will be employed to mitigate orbital debris. Mitigation of Orbital Debris, Second Report and Order, 19 FCC Rcd 11567 (2004). 
  The Commission adopted its orbital debris mitigation rules based on a determination that orbital debris could affect the cost, reliability, continuity and safety of satellite operations and therefore has bearing on Congress’s mandate that the FCC encourage “the larger and more effective use of radio in the public interest.” Id. at 11575, para. 14 (citing 47 U.S.C. § 303(g)).
  Commenters raise general concerns regarding orbital debris mitigation for SpaceX’s constellation, including concerns about collision risk, disposal failures, and casualty risk. See e.g., Viasat Petition at 7-10; Amazon Petition at 5-8; SWF Comments at 1.  Because of the high volume of comments related to orbital debris concerns that were submitted in response to the application, we cite to illustrative examples of these concerns represented in petitions to deny rather than to a string citation of numerous commenters raising the same concerns.  In addition, comments also express concern about orbital congestion posed by one million ODCs, see e.g., Comments of Colin Cooper, Collin Pierson, Connie Levesque, Daniel Opdyke, and Jeremy Lavina, but the Commission’s rules do not place limits on the number of space stations that can be authorized with similar orbital parameters and the Space Bureau declines to invent a de facto rule in this adjudicatory licensing proceeding. 
   
11. We find that SpaceX’s orbital debris mitigation plan for Starmind satellites that will operate below 600 km complies with the Commission’s orbital debris mitigation rules.  SpaceX has provided the information about its orbital debris mitigation strategies for these satellites that is required under our rules 47 CFR § 25.114(d)(14).
 and will comply with Commission rules regarding orbital debris mitigation, including post-mission disposal. Id., § 25.283(e).
  In addition, SpaceX has committed to providing additional orbital debris mitigation information for Starmind satellites operating above 600 km, SpaceX May 29, 2026 Letter at 5-6.
 including additional information supporting its request for waiver of the Commission’s post-mission disposal requirements for satellites operating in or passing through LEO, below 2,000 km. 47 CFR § 25.114(d)(14)(vii)(D)(1) (“For space stations . . . ending their mission below 2000 km altitude, successful disposal is defined, for the purposes of this paragraph (d)(14)(vii)(D)(1), as atmospheric re-entry of the spacecraft as soon as practicable, but no later than five years following completion of the mission.”).
  We therefore condition this grant on SpaceX filing an application for modification containing its final orbital debris mitigation plan, responsive to all elements of the Commission’s rules, prior to deploying any Starmind satellites that will operate at altitudes above 600 km. D. Issues Beyond the Scope of FCC Authority
1. Protection of the Human Environment
14. Consistent with the Commission’s October 2026 Report and Order in the Modernizing the Commission’s National Environmental Policy Rules Act Rules proceeding (NEPA Order), we will not require further environmental review under the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) for SpaceX’s Starmind system. See Modernizing the Commission’s National Environmental Policy Act Rules, Report and Order and Further Notice of Proposed Rulemaking, FCC 26-64 at paras. 62-69 (rel. Oct. 1, 2026) (NEPA Order).  We note that the new rules do not come into effect until 30 days after publication in the Federal Register or after conclusion of the Paperwork Reduction Act process, but even applying our current rule framework, we decline to require further environmental review under NEPA.
  The NEPA Order concluded that Commission-licensed space-based operations do not constitute a major Federal action (MFA) as “extraterritorial activities,” and thus do not trigger the agency’s NEPA obligations. Id. at para. 62. 
  The Commission held that since space-based activities do not take place within U.S. territory, such extraterrestrial activities are outside the scope of U.S. jurisdiction. Id. 
  Further, spectrum authorizations for space-based operations do not create “Federal control and responsibility” over space-based operations, and ultimately, licensees retain discretion and control over the deployment and operation of their space systems. Id. at para. 69.  
  Consistent with the NEPA Order, we dismiss arguments on the record that the Commission must conduct an environmental review of the proposed ODC system pursuant to its NEPA obligations. See e.g., AAS Petition at 9-10; Dark Sky Comments at 8-9; 
 
15. Even if we had jurisdiction to review potential environmental impacts of the Starmind system, we find that concerns related to the potential atmospheric risks of the Starmind system and the resulting effects on the human environment do not require the Commission to conduct further environmental review under NEPA. Dark Sky Comments at 7.  
  As noted in prior orders, the FAA is the agency responsible for conducting review of the environmental effects of rocket launches and has undertaken review of potential impacts from SpaceX’s and other company’s launches, and under NEPA, an agency does not need to duplicate environmental review undertaken by another agency. See Space Exploration Holdings, LLC, Request for Orbital Deployment and Operating Authority for the SpaceX Gen2 NGSO Satellite System, Order and Authorization, 37 FCC Rcd 14882, 14933-34 (2022).  Although prior to the recent Commission NEPA Order, the Commission at the time also found that the concerns raised on the record did not warrant environmental review under NEPA.  Id. at 14935-36.
  Potential concerns related to the emissions of rocket launches and the subsequent impacts on the human environment as an indirect result of the Starmind system are outside of the Commission’s purview. See, e.g., Dark Sky Comments at 7 (raising concerns that exhaust emissions from frequent launches would add ozone-depleting pollutants to the atmosphere and could increase risk of harm to the human environment).  See also Comments of Central Arkansas Astronomical Society, Alexandra Tyukavina, Jean G. Hubbell, Dr. Alan George Fowler, Ellen Jones, and Jeremy Pine (referencing lithium pollution allegedly caused by a failed Falcon 9 launch vehicle).
  Other concerns, including impacts on the atmosphere caused by satellites burning up on re-entry, remain speculative and not supported in the record. See e.g., Dark Sky Comments; AAS Petition.
